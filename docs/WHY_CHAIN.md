# Why-chain (make-me-an-expert gate), W01 The Falling Satellite

The expert's first question: **"If gravity pulls everything down, why doesn't a
satellite fall down?"** Every step below answers one why and raises the next.
Each link was checked for guesses a newcomer would have to make.

1. Everything falls. Earth pulls things toward its middle; that pull is
   gravity. (No leap: the pull is experienced daily, just named.)
   Raises: does the pull reach as high as satellites fly?
2. A sideways throw still falls; it just travels while it falls. Sideways
   motion and falling are independent. (No leap: both throws land; only the
   landing spot moves. The independence is stated, then shown.)
   Raises: what if it travelled so far that the ground itself dropped away?
3. Earth is round, so its surface curves away. At about 8 km/s the ground
   curves away as fast as the ball falls: it falls all the way around. That IS
   an orbit. (Gap checked: a real surface throw would hit air first, so the
   pretend 400 km mountain above the air is declared, not smuggled.)
   Raises: so is there gravity up there or not?
4. Gravity up there is about 9/10 of ground strength. Floating astronauts are
   falling too, alongside their station. (Gap checked: "weightless = no
   gravity" is named as the misconception and answered directly.)
   Raises: do all orbits take the same time?
5. Close is quick, far is slow: about 90 minutes low, about a day very far.
   (Gap checked: WHY far means slow is in Read a little more: weaker pull
   needs less sideways speed, so each circle takes longer.)
   Raises: what is the ground doing while satellites circle?
6. Earth spins once a day. A quick low satellite sweeps over place after
   place. (New instrument introduced before use: the riding-with-Earth panel.)
   Raises: could a satellite keep pace with the spin?
7. A one-day orbit above the equator, going the way Earth turns, stays over
   one spot: geostationary, the special geosynchronous case.
   Raises: does ANY one-day orbit stay fixed?
8. No. A tilted one-day orbit drifts north and south, drawing a loop over the
   day. Same period, not the same place.
9. Transfer: a fresh tilt the child has not watched. Predict fixed or
   wandering, and say why. (Expert pattern analysis: read the period and the
   tilt, predict the ground track — the same reading a real ground-track
   analyst does, at age-appropriate depth.)

How an expert approaches the input, stated at child level: an orbit expert
asks two things first — how long does one circle take, and is the path flat
over the equator or tilted? Steps 7-9 train exactly that reading.

Known limits declared to the learner: drawings not to scale; speeds/times
real; no air below the pretend mountain; one "day" = one Earth spin (~23 h 56
min); perfect circles only.
